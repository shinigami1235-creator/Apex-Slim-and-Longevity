Longevity / IV Therapy

IV Therapy

An IV drip puts vitamins, minerals and fluid straight into your blood. Vitamins you take by mouth go through your gut first, and your body can only absorb so much at a time.

A study at the US National Institutes of Health gave people 1.25 g of vitamin C by drip and by mouth. The drip raised the level in the blood to 885 µmol/L at its peak, more than six times the 135 µmol/L from swallowing it. A drip helps after fluid loss and for a proven lack of a vitamin, while the evidence for energy, recovery and skin is thin.

When a drip helps

A drip replaces fluid and salts quickly after vomiting, diarrhoea, heavy sweating or a fever, when it's hard to drink enough. It also treats a proven lack of a vitamin or mineral, especially in people whose gut doesn't absorb well, like after weight-loss surgery or with a bowel condition. There is strong medical evidence for these uses.

Where the evidence is thin

People also come in for drips for energy, to recover after a long flight or a hard week, or for their skin. A lot of them say they feel better afterwards, but few large trials have compared a real drip with a fake one, so nobody knows yet how much it helps healthy people. Doctors at Cleveland Clinic say its value as a general wellness treatment is uncertain.

No good studies show yet that a drip removes toxins or slows ageing.

What's in the drip

The base is saline with salts called electrolytes. The doctor adds the vitamins and minerals for your goal, most often B vitamins, vitamin C, magnesium and zinc, and decides the dose after your health check.

A hydration drip gives you fluid and electrolytes after a stomach bug, a fever or a day out in the heat. A vitamin B drip is for people with low vitamin B12, which is more common in vegetarians, older adults and people on some long-term medicines. A vitamin C drip is for people with low vitamin C or when the doctor wants a higher level than tablets can reach. A blood test shows whether you're low in a vitamin, and the doctor uses it to decide what goes in your drip.

Your visit

The doctor asks about your health and any medicines you take and checks your blood pressure. A nurse puts a thin tube into a vein in your arm, and the drip runs for 30 to 60 minutes while you sit and read or use your phone. You can go back to your day as soon as the tube comes out.

Risks

  • bruising, soreness or swelling where the needle went in
  • irritation of the vein
  • infection where the needle went in, which is rare when it's done cleanly
  • getting too much of a vitamin or mineral, or salts going out of balance
  • too much fluid for people with heart or kidney disease

Tell the doctor first

Tell the doctor before your first drip if you have heart disease, kidney disease or high blood pressure, or if you're pregnant or breastfeeding.

G6PD deficiency is common in Thailand. In people who have it, a high dose of vitamin C by drip can break down red blood cells, so the doctor may test you for it first.

Questions

How often can I have one?

It depends on your goal and your blood tests. A drip for dehydration is often a one-off. For a vitamin deficiency you'll have a course of drips until your levels are back to normal.

Is a drip better than taking vitamins?

If you're healthy and eat well, food and tablets give you what you need every day. A drip helps when you need fluid fast, when your gut can't absorb enough, or when the doctor wants a higher level than tablets can give.

Does it hurt?

It stings a little when the needle goes in, and the fluid can feel cold in your arm.

Can I drive after?

Yes. You can drive and go back to work as soon as it's done.
