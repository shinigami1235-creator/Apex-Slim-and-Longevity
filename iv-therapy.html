Longevity / IV Therapy

IV Therapy

Vitamins you swallow pass through your gut first, and your body only absorbs so much at a time. An IV drip puts vitamins, minerals and fluid straight into your blood.

The difference can be large. In a study at the US National Institutes of Health, 1.25 g of vitamin C reached a peak of 885 µmol/L in the blood when given by drip and 135 µmol/L when swallowed, more than six times higher. A higher blood level is still only a number, so this page sets out where a drip has good evidence behind it and where the evidence is thin.

Where a drip clearly helps

A drip replaces fluid and salts quickly after vomiting, diarrhoea, heavy sweating or a fever, when drinking enough is hard. It also treats a proven lack of a vitamin or mineral, especially in people whose gut absorbs poorly, such as after weight-loss surgery or with a bowel condition. These uses have strong medical evidence behind them.

Where the evidence is still thin

People also book drips for more energy, recovery after a long flight or a heavy week, and better skin. Many say they feel better afterwards, while large trials comparing a real drip with a fake one are few, so the size of the benefit for healthy people is not yet known. Doctors at Cleveland Clinic call its value as a general wellness tool uncertain, and the doctor at your consult tells you the same.

Claims that a drip clears toxins from the body or slows ageing are not backed by good studies yet.

What goes in the bag

The base is a saline fluid with salts called electrolytes. The doctor adds vitamins and minerals for your goal, most often B vitamins, vitamin C, magnesium and zinc, and sets the dose from your health check.

Common drips and what they are for

  • Hydration drip. Fluid and electrolytes after a stomach bug, a fever or a day in the heat.
  • Vitamin B drip. For people with low vitamin B12 levels, which is more common in vegetarians, older adults and people on some long-term medicines.
  • Vitamin C drip. For people with a low vitamin C level, or when the doctor wants a higher level than tablets can give.

A blood test shows whether you are low in a vitamin, and the doctor uses it to decide what goes in your drip.

Your visit

The doctor asks about your health and medicines and checks your blood pressure. A nurse places a thin tube in a vein in your arm, and the drip runs for 30 to 60 minutes while you sit back, read or use your phone. You can go back to your day as soon as the tube comes out.

Risks

  • Bruising, soreness or swelling where the needle went in
  • Irritation of the vein
  • Infection at the needle site, which is rare with clean technique
  • Too much of a vitamin or mineral, or salts out of balance
  • Fluid overload in people with heart or kidney disease

Check with the doctor first

Tell the doctor before your first drip if you have heart disease, kidney disease or high blood pressure, or if you are pregnant or breastfeeding.

G6PD deficiency is common in Thailand, and high-dose vitamin C by drip can break down red blood cells in people who have it. The doctor may test for it before giving you a high-dose vitamin C drip.

Questions

How often can I have one?

The doctor sets how often from your goal and your blood tests. A drip for dehydration is often a one-off, and a drip for a deficiency follows a course until your levels are back to normal.

Is a drip better than taking vitamins?

For a healthy person who eats well, food and tablets cover daily needs. A drip helps when you need fluid fast, when your gut cannot absorb enough, or when the doctor wants a level that tablets cannot reach.

Does it hurt?

You feel a small sting as the needle goes in, and the fluid can feel cool in your arm while it runs.

Can I drive afterwards?

Yes. You can drive and go back to work as soon as the drip is finished.
